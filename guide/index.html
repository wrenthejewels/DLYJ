How I Built This Model

This model was built iteratively. Each version's abstraction was too coarse somewhere; the fix was always to move one layer lower. This page reconstructs that process: the wrong turns, the structural insights, and the reasons behind every major design choice.

The Starting Problem

The question this model tries to answer is not "which occupations are exposed to AI?"

It is: "If AI gets better at parts of a role, what happens to the role as an organizational unit?"

These sound similar. They are not. The first question asks about technological overlap: which tasks can a machine do? The second asks about organizational consequences: what happens to the seat when some of the work inside it gets cheaper?

That distinction matters because a pure exposure score collapses several structurally different outcomes into a single number:

  • Augmentation. The role becomes more productive. The human stays, does more, and possibly becomes more valuable. The seat does not shrink.
  • Compression. The routine layer of the role gets automated. The human still does the judgment-heavy core, but the organization needs fewer people doing it. The seat shrinks but does not disappear.
  • Splitting. The role fractures into a cheaper execution tier (partially automated) and a smaller oversight or judgment tier (retained or elevated). Two different jobs replace one.
  • Collapse. The role loses enough of its structural reason to exist that the seat is eliminated or absorbed into an adjacent role.

An exposure score that says "this occupation is 65% exposed to AI" cannot distinguish between these outcomes. It tells you that something is likely to change. It cannot tell you what changes, how it changes, or whether the person still has a job afterward.

This is not a minor limitation. It is a category error. "AI can do some tasks in this occupation" is a statement about technological capability. "This job disappears" is a statement about organizational decision-making, adoption economics, institutional structure, and the role's remaining human value. The gap between those two statements is where the interesting, decision-relevant analysis lives.

The model exists because that gap is not being addressed by most of the work in this space.

What Everyone Else Built

Before explaining what this model does differently, it is worth understanding what the existing research actually measures, because it is better than the popular summaries suggest, and also more limited in ways that matter.

The lineage

The modern wave of "AI and jobs" research begins with Frey and Osborne (2013), who trained a Gaussian process classifier on 70 hand-labeled occupations to estimate automation probabilities for 702 O*NET occupations. The headline finding, that 47% of U.S. employment was at "high risk," became one of the most cited numbers in labor economics. It also became one of the most misunderstood.

Their method was reasonable for what it was: a first pass at ranking occupations by technological susceptibility. They identified three "engineering bottlenecks" to computerization (perception and manipulation, creative intelligence, and social intelligence) and selected 9 O*NET variables to proxy for those bottlenecks. The classifier generalized from the labeled examples to score every occupation.

The problems were structural, not just methodological:

  • The 70 training labels were subjective expert judgments. Only nine of 246 available O*NET variables were selected, with limited justification for the selection.
  • The model scored entire occupations as automatable or not. It did not decompose roles into tasks, so it could not represent partial automation or role restructuring.
  • The OECD's task-level correction (Arntz, Gregory & Zierahn, 2016) applied Frey and Osborne's logic to tasks instead of occupations and found that only 9% of OECD jobs were at high risk, dramatically lower than 47%. Same basic methodology, different unit of analysis, very different headline.

The lesson was not that Frey and Osborne were wrong. It was that the unit of analysis changes the answer, and occupation-level analysis systematically overstates risk because it treats the entire job as one indivisible block.

The next generation

The subsequent wave of research moved toward task-level analysis, which was a genuine improvement:

  • Felten, Raj & Seamans (AIOE) mapped 10 specific AI application areas (image recognition, translation, reading comprehension, etc.) to 52 human abilities in O*NET. The exposure score reflects how much overlap exists between what AI applications can do and what abilities an occupation requires. This is a capability-overlap measure, not a prediction of job loss.
  • Webb (2020) used NLP to match verb-noun pairs from patent text with verb-noun pairs from O*NET task descriptions. The key finding: unlike software and industrial robots (which historically targeted routine and low-skill work), AI patents are directed at high-skill tasks. This confirmed that AI's labor market impact would be qualitatively different from previous automation waves.
  • Brynjolfsson, Mitchell & Rock (SML) developed a 23-question rubric scoring each of 18,156 O*NET tasks on suitability for machine learning. Their important contribution was noting explicitly that most occupations have some ML-suitable tasks, very few are fully automatable, and realizing ML's potential typically requires job redesign rather than simple substitution.
  • Eloundou et al. (GPTs are GPTs, 2023) combined human expert annotations with GPT-4's own classifications to assess LLM exposure at the task level. The finding: ~15% of all U.S. worker tasks could be done significantly faster with an LLM alone; this rises to 47–56% when including LLM-powered tools. About 80% of the workforce could have at least 10% of their tasks affected.

The most recent and methodologically distinct entry is the Anthropic Economic Index (2025–2026), which measures actual observed AI usage rather than theoretical exposure. It uses a privacy-preserving analysis system called Clio to map millions of real Claude.ai conversations to O*NET's ~20,000 task descriptions. By the January 2026 report, they had introduced five "economic primitives" (task complexity, skill levels, use case, AI autonomy, success rate) and could distinguish augmentation from automation at the conversation level. This is the closest the field has come to measuring what AI is actually doing to work rather than what it could do.

What they all share

Every study in this lineage is doing a version of the same thing: measuring technological overlap between AI capabilities and work tasks. Some do it more carefully than others. Some use theoretical assessments, others use observed behavior. All of them are stronger than popular coverage suggests.

But they also share a common structural limitation: none of them model the full causal chain from "AI can perform a task" to "this changes the labor market."

The chain runs through organizational decision-making, adoption costs, regulatory constraints, worker adaptation, demand effects, job redesign, and institutional structure. Every exposure model measures the input to that chain and implicitly treats it as the output.

This is why the Yale Budget Lab, reviewing the field in 2025, found that individual AI exposure scores have poor predictive power for actual labor market outcomes. The various measures also disagree with each other substantially, with disagreement increasing at higher exposure levels, precisely where precision matters most.

The existing research is good at what it does: ranking occupations and tasks by technological overlap with AI. It is weak at what people use it for: predicting what actually happens to jobs. The gap between those two things is not a minor caveat. It is the central unsolved problem in the field.

Why Exposure Scores Are Not Enough

The problem with exposure scores is not that they are inaccurate. Many of them are carefully constructed. The problem is that they answer the wrong question. Or, more precisely, they answer a question that is much less useful than the one people think they are answering.

Here are the specific failure modes.

Failure 1: They cannot distinguish core work from support work

Exposure scores treat every task in a role as equally important to the role's survival. But roles are not flat lists of tasks. Some tasks are the reason the seat exists. Others exist only to support those core tasks.

A financial analyst spends significant time on data gathering, spreadsheet maintenance, and report formatting. Those tasks are highly exposed to AI. But the role exists because someone needs to exercise judgment about capital allocation, frame uncertainty for decision-makers, and translate analytical findings into stakeholder-ready recommendations. If AI handles the spreadsheets, the analyst role does not vanish. It recomposes around the judgment work that was always the point.

An exposure score cannot see this. It weights a data-gathering task and a judgment task equally because it has no concept of why the role exists.

Failure 2: They cannot model spillover

Some tasks are not easy to automate directly. But they become less valuable when adjacent work is compressed or automated. An office coordinator whose main job is routing information between teams loses value not because AI can route information (it can, somewhat) but because the upstream work that generates the information flow is itself being restructured.

This is indirect pressure, and it is invisible to any model that scores tasks independently. Without a dependency graph (explicit edges connecting tasks that support, review, or enable each other), a model systematically understates risk to support work and overstates the autonomy of each task.

Failure 3: They cannot explain within-occupation variation

Two accountants at different firms may do very different work. One spends 80% of their time on transaction processing and routine reconciliation. The other spends 80% on advisory work, exception handling, and control interpretation. An occupation-level exposure score gives them the same number.

This is not a minor edge case. The Autor task framework (2013) emphasizes that within-occupation task variation is large and economically significant. Census ACS microdata confirms this: many occupations show substantial wage, education, and industry dispersion, which is a strong signal that one occupation label covers structurally different roles.

Any model that assigns one score per occupation is implicitly assuming that all workers in that occupation do the same work in the same proportions. That assumption is wrong, and it gets more wrong for exactly the occupations where the answer matters most: the large, heterogeneous professional roles where some variants are under much more pressure than others.

Failure 4: They conflate "tasks get cheaper" with "the role disappears"

This is the deepest problem. It is the one that motivated building this model.

When AI makes some tasks cheaper, several things can happen:

  • The remaining human tasks become more valuable because they are now the binding constraint. A senior developer whose routine coding is automated becomes more valuable as a system architect and technical decision-maker, not less.
  • The organization restructures the role around the retained human work. The seat changes shape but does not disappear.
  • Demand for the output increases because it is now cheaper to produce, creating more work for the humans who oversee and judge the output. This is the Jevons paradox applied to labor.
  • The role splits into tiers: a cheaper execution layer (partially automated) and a smaller but higher-value oversight layer. Total headcount may drop, but the human role is not eliminated. It is restructured.

Exposure scores cannot represent any of these dynamics because they have no model of what the role is beyond a bag of tasks. They cannot tell you whether the remaining tasks hold together as a coherent job, whether the human still owns something that cannot be delegated, or whether the organizational structure even permits full delegation.

Failure 5: They ignore organizational adoption friction

The gap between "AI can do a task" and "a firm automates that task" is enormous. It includes:

  • Economic feasibility. Automation has to be cheaper than labor, including implementation, maintenance, error correction, and the cost of getting it wrong.
  • Regulatory constraints. Many decisions in law, medicine, finance, and compliance require human sign-off regardless of AI capability. A lawyer's advice function cannot be delegated to AI by institutional definition, regardless of how well the AI reasons about law.
  • Organizational inertia. Firms adopt technology much more slowly than the technology becomes available. Workflow integration, change management, and employee training are real bottlenecks.
  • Institutional trust. Some work requires a human because the stakeholder needs to trust a human, not because the task is technically hard. Fiduciary responsibility, patient relationships, and regulatory accountability are structural, not contingent on AI capability.

Exposure models systematically ignore these friction sources because they measure what AI can do, not what organizations will do. The Dallas Fed, reviewing the evidence in early 2026, noted that AI is "simultaneously aiding and replacing workers." The same technology augments some roles while displacing others, even within the same occupation. A single score cannot represent this.

The upshot

The task-based approach from labor economics (Autor, 2013) is the right starting frame: analyze roles as bundles of tasks, not indivisible blocks. But tasks alone are still not roles. A role is a bundle of tasks organized around a function that someone is accountable for. To model what happens to the role, you need to model the function and the accountability structure, not just the tasks.

That insight drove the rest of the build.

From Occupations to Clusters

The natural first model was an occupation-level exposure score: map you to an occupation, assign a number. This is what most of the research described above produces, and it is useful for ranking. It is weak for explanation.

The core problem is that an occupation-level score treats the role as an indivisible atom. It cannot show which parts of the work are under pressure. It cannot model why a financial analyst and a financial analyst at a different firm face different levels of risk even though they share a job title. It cannot represent the difference between "your spreadsheet work is exposed" and "your judgment work is exposed," because it has flattened both into a single number.

The first structural move was to decompose each occupation into a weighted bundle of 12 task clusters (or task families). Each cluster carries its own automation-difficulty estimate and its own wave assignment. Instead of saying "this occupation is 55% exposed," the model could now say "the documentation cluster is in the current wave, the analysis cluster is in the next wave, and the stakeholder-management cluster is in the distant wave."

This was a pragmatic choice for several reasons:

  • Cluster priors are easier to review than task-level scores. With 12 clusters, a human reviewer can check each one. With 846 task rows, that gets harder.
  • Cluster priors are more stable than sparse task-level evidence. When you only have strong evidence for a few tasks, the cluster average gives you a workable fallback rather than noise.
  • Clusters make the wave model interpretable. Pressure starts in different parts of the role at different times. Clusters are the natural unit for "this work is under pressure now" versus "this work is under pressure later."

But clusters have a fundamental weakness: they are still averages. Tasks inside a cluster can differ materially in how exposed they are, how important they are to the role, and how much human leverage they carry. A cluster called "analysis and research" might contain both "gather publicly available data" (highly automatable) and "exercise judgment about ambiguous client situations" (not automatable in the same way). The cluster average papers over that distinction.

So cluster priors were a workable scaffold, not an end state. They solved the decomposition problem without solving the granularity problem.

From Clusters to Tasks

The next bottleneck was that the model needed a better representation of the role itself. Clusters group tasks, but the model could not yet reason about individual tasks, their relative importance, or their relationships to each other.

O*NET provides task statements for every occupation, but they were not sufficient for many of the occupations the model needed to cover. Some occupations had only 20 task rows, which is thin enough that the cluster composition becomes fragile. Many modern workflow tasks (managing Slack channels, triaging JIRA tickets, running sprint retrospectives) were missing entirely because O*NET descriptions lag actual practice by years.

So the model expanded the task layer with several additions:

  • Reviewed job-description task additions that filled gaps where O*NET underdescribed a role
  • Stable internal task IDs so a task could be tracked, edited, and scored consistently even when public sources described the same work differently
  • Value centrality: how much each task explains why the role exists. A task with high value centrality is one that, if removed, would call into question whether the seat is still needed.
  • Bargaining power weight: how much worker leverage depends on performing this task. Some tasks are important to the organization but do not give the worker much leverage. Others are the reason the worker is hard to replace.
  • Role criticality: whether the task is core, supporting, or optional to the role's structural identity.

This mattered because once tasks became first-class objects with their own attributes, the model could stop asking "how exposed is this occupation?" and start asking a much more useful question: "Which specific tasks explain why this role exists, and which of those tasks are reachable by AI?"

That is a different question with a different answer. An occupation can be 60% exposed at the task level and still structurally sound if the exposed tasks are all supporting work and the core tasks that define the role are in the distant wave. Conversely, an occupation that is only 30% exposed can be structurally fragile if the exposed tasks happen to be the ones that explain why the seat exists.

Task-level attributes made that distinction visible.

From Tasks to a Graph

With tasks as nodes, the missing piece was edges.

Real work is not a flat list of independent activities. Tasks depend on each other. Some tasks exist mainly to enable other tasks. Some tasks review the output of other tasks. Some tasks only have value because upstream work produces the input they consume.

The model added a dependency graph with 1,397 explicit edges across 34 occupations. Each edge connects a source task to a target task with a dependency type (supports, reviews, enables, feeds, follows_from) and a strength weight.

This introduced one of the most important conceptual upgrades in the whole system: the distinction between direct pressure and indirect pressure.

  • Direct pressure means AI can perform the task itself. The task is technologically reachable.
  • Indirect pressure means the task becomes less necessary because adjacent work changes. The task may not be automatable at all, but it loses value when the upstream work it supports is compressed or eliminated.

This distinction matters enormously for support work. Consider an office coordinator whose primary tasks include routing information between teams, scheduling follow-up meetings based on project updates, and maintaining status trackers. None of those tasks are particularly easy to automate directly. They require context, judgment about priorities, and organizational knowledge.

But if the upstream project work is restructured by AI, if status updates are generated automatically, if meeting coordination is handled by scheduling tools, then the coordinator's tasks do not get "automated" in the naive sense. They get devalued. The work that made the coordinator's tasks necessary has shifted, and the support tasks lose their structural reason to exist.

Without the dependency graph, the model would score that coordinator's tasks independently and conclude that they are resistant to automation. With the graph, it can trace the indirect pressure from upstream changes and arrive at a more accurate assessment.

The reverse is also true: some tasks gain value when adjacent tasks are exposed. If AI handles routine data preparation, the analyst's interpretation and judgment tasks become the binding constraint, and their value increases. The graph captures both directions.

Why this mattered

Without the graph, the model would systematically understate risk to support work and overstate the autonomy of each task. Support work is not independently hard to automate; it is structurally coupled to the work it supports. If you miss that coupling, you miss some of the most important dynamics in role transformation.

Why Tasks Are Still Not Enough

At this point the model had a task graph with dependency edges, scored tasks with direct and indirect pressure, and per-task attributes like value centrality and bargaining power. That was already much better than an occupation-level exposure score.

But it was still missing something fundamental.

The problem is this: tasks describe how work gets done. They do not describe what the human role is there to own.

That distinction is concrete. "AI can write first drafts of legal briefs" is a statement about task exposure. "The legal system still requires a human attorney to own the representation" is a statement about institutional structure, accountability, and delegation authority. They live in different layers of the problem.

What functions represent

The model added a function layer on top of the task graph. A function is not a task. It is the durable organizational purpose that a bundle of tasks supports. Functions answer the question: if all the exposed tasks got cheaper tomorrow, would a coherent human-owned responsibility still remain?

Some examples from the model's actual function data:

  • A compliance officer's primary function is control_assurance: "Interpret policies, laws, and controls so operations remain compliant and auditable." The officer also has a supplemental issue_remediation function for complaints, evidence readiness, and issue closure. Many of the tasks under those functions are exposed (document review, report generation, checklist verification). But the function of interpreting controls and making compliance decisions cannot be delegated to AI by institutional definition. The role recomposes; it does not collapse.
  • A software developer's primary function is software_delivery: "Translate product and system needs into working software." The developer also has a supplemental technical_stewardship function for standards, architecture, and technical direction. Routine coding tasks are highly exposed. But the stewardship function, which carries judgment of 0.84 and authority of 0.44, survives because someone still needs to own technical decisions that shape the system's long-term trajectory.
  • A paralegal's primary function is legal_support: legal research coordination, matter tracking, and workflow under attorney direction. The paralegal also has a supplemental procedural_execution function for filing, drafting, and procedural follow-through. The execution function carries low authority (0.16). The legal support function carries moderate authority (0.30) but sits under attorney sign-off. The task exposure is high; the retained function is real but supervised. The role compresses toward the coordination work, not toward elimination.

Why a task-only model gets this wrong

A task-only model would look at a paralegal with 60% of tasks exposed and conclude the role is under severe displacement pressure. The function layer adds crucial context: yes, the execution tasks are exposed, but the legal-support function that justifies the seat is retained under attorney direction, and institutional structure (bar rules, court procedures, liability allocation) prevents full delegation regardless of AI capability.

The same logic applies to accountants, financial analysts, compliance officers, and many professional roles. The tasks are exposed. The functions are retained. The role changes shape but does not disappear.

Without the function layer, the model would systematically overstate displacement risk for professional and judgment-heavy roles. With it, the model can distinguish between "the work gets cheaper" and "the seat goes away."

The accountability dimensions

Each function in the model carries an explicit accountability profile with five dimensions:

  • Judgment requirement (0 to 1): How much interpretive discretion, contextual reasoning, or tradeoff decision-making the function demands. A lawyer's advice function scores 0.96. A transaction processor scores much lower.
  • Human authority requirement (0 to 1): Whether someone in the role must hold formal decision-making power or sign-off authority. Lawyers score 0.94 because they sign legal documents and own representation decisions. Paralegals score 0.30 because they operate under attorney direction.
  • Trust requirement (0 to 1): Whether the work depends on stakeholder, client, or institutional trust in a specific human. This is not "trust that the person is competent." It is about whether the institutional relationship requires a named human accountable party.
  • Regulatory liability weight (0 to 1): Whether the role carries legal, compliance, or institutional liability for decisions. Lawyers carry 0.92. Administrative clerks carry 0.04.
  • Delegability guardrail (computed): The institutional friction against simply assigning the function to AI. This is the composite signal that captures whether judgment, authority, trust, or liability structurally prevent full delegation, regardless of AI capability.

These dimensions are not abstract quality scores. They are structural properties of the role's position in an organization. A high delegability guardrail means the function cannot be handed off to AI because of institutional, legal, or organizational constraints, not because the AI is not good enough yet.

How functions change the model's conclusions

The function layer flows into the model through several paths:

Retained function strength measures how much of the role's human-owned purpose survives after task exposure. The formula weights delegability guardrails at 42% of the signal, because institutional structure is the most durable source of human retention. A role with high delegability guardrails retains function strength even when most of its tasks are exposed.

Retained accountability strength is a six-term formula combining delegability (20%), human authority (22%), judgment (18%), unexposed share (18%), trust (12%), and regulatory liability (10%). This measures whether the role still carries real human decision ownership, not just residual tasks.

Coupling protection flows into the wave difficulty calculation. Functions add friction to automation difficulty through the formula's coupling term (weighted at 10%). Higher-function roles are harder to fully automate because the role's structural obligations create friction even when task-level work is exposed.

These signals feed directly into role fate classification. The classifier uses retained leverage, residual role integrity, exposed core share, and wave trajectory to sort roles into seven states: augmented, compressed, elevated, split, expanded, collapsed, or mixed transition. Without the function layer, it would only have task exposure and wave timing to work with, and it would systematically misclassify professional roles as facing worse outcomes than they actually face.

The key insight

A task-only model asks: "What percentage of work can AI do?" The function layer adds: "What percentage of human responsibility, judgment, and authority does the role actually lose?" When those two questions have different answers, the role survives in some form. This is the step that turned the system from an exposure model into a role-transformation model.

Making It Real

By this point the model had a task graph, dependency edges, a function layer, and accountability profiles. The structure was much better than an occupation-level score. But it still assumed that you matched a generic occupation bundle, and it still leaned too hard on cluster-level priors rather than task-level evidence.

Two problems remained: personalization and evidence quality.

Editable role composition

Real users do not match generic occupation bundles. A software developer at a startup does different work than a software developer at a bank. An accountant focused on audit work has a structurally different role from an accountant focused on financial reporting. The model needed to let users edit the role structure itself, not just answer a few questions about their situation.

The editable composition layer added:

  • Source-bucketed tasks you can add or remove
  • Editable function anchors that change which functions the role is scored against
  • Optional user-declared support links between tasks
  • Optional task-to-function link overrides
  • Reviewed role variants for the most heterogeneous occupations (currently 7), so the model can recommend a starting baseline that better matches your actual work

This moved personalization from perturbing top-level coefficients to editing the actual graph being scored. The user changes the structure; the model re-scores the structure. That is a much more direct interaction than "answer five questions and we will adjust your number."

The evidence problem

Even after the task graph and function layers existed, the scoring engine was still mostly projecting cluster-level difficulty estimates onto individual tasks. That is a reasonable fallback when task-level evidence is thin, but it is not the intended end state.

The model needed to be able to use task-level evidence when it was good, and fall back to cluster priors when it was not. That led to the five-tier evidence resolver:

  1. Live task evidence from the Anthropic Economic Index (actual observed AI usage)
  2. Reviewed task estimates from manual expert review
  3. Benchmark task labels from published research (GPTs are GPTs, SML, etc.)
  4. Cluster prior proxies that attach cluster-level estimates to tasks
  5. Fallback task proxies when nothing better is available

The resolver tries each tier in order and uses the best available evidence. This means the model's confidence varies by task: some tasks are scored with direct empirical evidence, others with reviewed estimates, and others with cluster fallbacks. The source tier is labeled on every task row, so you (and any auditor) can see exactly where each score comes from.

This design reflects a core belief: weak evidence clearly labeled is better than strong-looking evidence from a hidden average. If a task's score comes from a cluster prior fallback, the model says so. It does not pretend to have task-level precision it does not have.

Why reviewed estimates outrank proxies, and sometimes outrank benchmark labels

A reviewed task estimate and a fallback proxy can both involve manual work, but they are not the same kind of claim. A reviewed task estimate says: “we looked at this exact task, in this exact occupation, and made a deliberate task-level judgment.” A proxy says: “we do not know this exact task well enough, so we are borrowing from broader structure” (usually the task cluster, occupation prior, or a similar-task fallback).

That is why reviewed task estimates rank above fallback proxies. They are narrower, more specific, and closer to the unit the model actually scores. Proxies are what keep the system stable when evidence is sparse, but they are still inherited structure rather than task-specific knowledge.

The same logic explains why a reviewed task estimate can outrank a benchmark task label, even when the benchmark source is empirical. A benchmark label such as GPTs are GPTs is still often broader than the exact task row and occupation context in this model. It may say that tasks of this kind are generally exposed, but it may not capture how that task behaves inside a particular occupation's function layer, support graph, or sign-off structure. A good reviewed estimate can therefore be the better fit for the exact row the engine is scoring.

So the resolver is not saying “our judgment is better because it is ours.” It is saying: prefer the source that makes the strongest claim about the exact task row being scored. First use live task evidence when it exists. If not, use a reviewed task-specific estimate. If not, use a benchmark task label. If not, inherit from broader structure. That is how the model tries to stay both empirically grounded and transparent about where it is still leaning on fallback structure.

Task-derived public outputs

The final piece was reversing the direction of aggregation. In earlier versions, cluster priors drove the public outputs (wave timing, role fate, cluster summaries). After the task layer matured, the model switched to aggregating scored task rows upward into cluster summaries, then recomputing wave timing from those task-derived clusters.

This was one of the most important architectural decisions in the system. The public outputs are now consequences of the task-level scoring, not inherited assumptions from a separate pre-task stack. What you see is derived from what the model actually scored, not from a parallel system running on different assumptions.

Where It Stands Now

By March 2026, the model had crossed an important line. It was no longer missing whole conceptual layers. The remaining work was mostly about making the existing layers more empirically grounded, less flattened, and easier to audit.

The current scoring path is an 11-step upward-aggregating pipeline:

  1. Map you to an occupation from 34 modeled occupations
  2. Build an editable task and function graph from 846 task rows and reviewed function anchors
  3. Compute baseline cluster difficulty from friction profiles and reviewed priors
  4. Project that baseline onto tasks
  5. Resolve task-level evidence from the five-tier task-source stack
  6. Let reliable resolved evidence alter task difficulty and direct pressure
  7. Propagate dependency spillover through 1,397 explicit edges
  8. Compute retained leverage per task
  9. Aggregate tasks back into cluster summaries
  10. Recompute wave timing from the task-derived cluster bundle
  11. Classify role fate using task, cluster, function, and wave signals

The direction of data flow matters. Earlier versions projected cluster priors downward onto tasks and then showed the cluster-level result. The current version scores tasks first, then aggregates upward into clusters, then into waves, then into role fate. What you see is a consequence of the task-level scoring, not a separate system running on different assumptions.

What this means

The model can now represent where pressure starts, how it spreads through dependencies, what work still holds bargaining power, and whether the human-owned role remains coherent. It is a structural role-transformation model, not just an exposure score.

What still looks strong

  • The runtime is task-scored rather than occupation-only
  • Public cluster and wave outputs are task-derived rather than inherited from a separate pre-task bundle
  • The task-source resolver is live and can promote reviewed and benchmark task evidence into scoring
  • The function layer is rich enough to express both explicit runtime variants and supplemental anchors without forcing every occupation into a binary split
  • The calibration layer is strong enough to separate high-confidence structural misses from weaker proxy disagreements

What is still unfinished

  • Task-first coverage is incomplete. The task-first scoring path works well for strongly evidenced tasks, but many tasks still start from cluster prior fallbacks. The model is not yet fully task-first everywhere.
  • Adoption realism is audited, not modeled. The calibration layer can flag when modeled adoption looks implausible against BTOS or other adoption signals. But the model does not yet predict organizational adoption timing directly. It models structural vulnerability and friction, not corporate decision-making.
  • Bargaining-power calibration is directionally useful but incomplete. The specialization lift and pressure-adjusted leverage formulas work for the strongest and weakest cases. The middle of the distribution is less well calibrated, because the external proxies (wages, education mix) are noisy indicators of role-level scarcity.
  • The explanation surface is better but not complete. The model now exposes per-task causal notes, source labels, evidence tiers, and a composition-edit delta. But it is not yet a full provenance browser where every intermediate score can be traced back to its inputs.
  • Most occupations still show one default baseline. Even when their function layer is richer than a single baseline implies, many occupations do not yet surface that richness in the visible explanation. The supplemental anchors are active in scoring but not always visible in the output narrative.

The remaining gap

The major remaining limitation is no longer basic architecture. It is a mix of evidence coverage, outer-layer adoption realism, explanation depth, and a few remaining structurally coarse occupations.

The practical next steps are:

  • Strengthen evidence coverage where the task-first path is still fallback-heavy
  • Keep adding supplemental anchors only where calibration says one function layer is still too flat
  • Improve the audit and explanation surface so the model is easier to defend
  • Treat BTOS and similar outer layers as calibration tools until they are strong enough to promote safely

The next best work is not "invent another abstraction." It is "make the existing abstractions more empirically grounded."

Two concrete examples of that work so far.

First: an OLS regression across 324 AEI task rows showed that the drafting and documentation clusters were the two largest systematic underestimates in the model, each running roughly 0.23 points below observed empirical difficulty. The root cause was that their friction profiles set judgment and tacit context dependence too low — treating these as mechanical work when quality drafting requires understanding organizational voice and audience purpose, and documentation requires regulatory context judgment and organizational knowledge. The regression could not reliably update the top-level formula weights (R²=0.105; coupling is collinear with the intercept; human advantage is confounded by how users select tasks on Claude), but the per-cluster gap was clear enough to act on directly. Both clusters now carry higher judgment and tacit values.

Second: the friction dimension weights themselves — how much each of the five friction signals contributes to automation difficulty — were previously set by intuition, with accountability weighted highest. Two independent external sources changed that. Dallas Fed research (February 2026) found wages rising specifically in AI-exposed occupations requiring tacit knowledge and experience, while employment fell where those qualities were absent. OECD job-posting analysis found that originality, which maps to judgment requirement, saw the largest skill demand increase in high-AI-exposure occupations. Both point to the same thing: tacit knowledge and judgment are the friction dimensions that actually protect roles in practice. The weights now reflect that — tacit (0.28) and judgment (0.26) together lead, accountability dropped from first to third (0.18).

Why Calibration Stays Outside Runtime

Once the model became task-first enough to produce meaningful disagreements with external data, the obvious question was: "Why not pour more outside data directly into the live score?"

The answer is that some data is better for calibration than for runtime. The intended separation is clean:

  • Runtime scoring uses task, function, and evidence layers directly tied to the role model. These are the signals that describe what the role is, how exposed its tasks are, and what human-owned functions remain.
  • Calibration uses external or non-runtime context to pressure-test those layers. These are the signals that tell you whether the model's structural assumptions look plausible against independent observations.

The separation matters because each layer measures something different. Task automability is not the same thing as organizational adoption. Organizational adoption is not the same thing as labor-market demand. Labor-market demand is not the same thing as occupational quality. If you blend them all into one score, the result becomes uninterpretable. You cannot tell whether a high score means "the tasks are exposed," "the industry is adopting AI fast," "the occupation is shrinking anyway," or some murky combination.

How calibration changes the runtime indirectly

Calibration does not leave the model unchanged. It changes the model by exposing where the model's own layers are structurally wrong. But the fix always goes into the model's own layers, not into the scoring formula as a direct external input.

The clearest examples from this model's history:

Bargaining power. The calibration queue flagged routine and support-heavy roles as having implausibly high retained bargaining power. Customer service representatives, bookkeeping clerks, and statistical assistants were all reading as though they had the kind of leverage associated with technically scarce or decision-owning roles. Instead of piping wage data or education data directly into the runtime, the bargaining-power formula itself was adjusted. It now leans more on pressure-adjusted retained leverage and less on static bargaining-weight averages. A separate specialization lift was added for high-knowledge technical roles (data scientists, software developers) whose scarcity was being understated by the same formula. The external calibration data identified the problem; the fix was internal.

Routine pressure. Once the calibration queue became strength-aware (distinguishing between strong structural checks and weaker contextual proxies), admin-heavy occupations like office clerks and secretaries moved to the top of the review list. The model was updated in two passes. First, the scorer learned to read existing routine-context signals more aggressively when estimating workflow compression. Second, a narrower clerical-execution context was added that fires only when the active role mix is dominated by workflow-admin, documentation, and execution-routine tasks at low authority. That extra context gives those task families a stronger pressure baseline without flattening higher-context admin roles the same way.

Accountability. The BLS Occupational Requirements Survey gave the model its strongest external read on human autonomy, supervisory responsibility, and pace control. When the ORS-backed queue was strong enough to trust, it exposed that the runtime was leaning too much on trust and liability when estimating retained accountability. The formula was adjusted to weight delegability guardrails (20%), human authority (22%), and judgment (18%) more heavily, while keeping trust (12%) and regulatory liability (10%) as smaller supporting terms. This made the accountability layer more about durable human sign-off and decision ownership, and less about any role that happens to sit in a trusted or regulated setting.

Function anchors. Many of the ~15 supplemental function anchors now in the model were not added through formula changes at all. They were added because calibration kept showing that one flat function layer was too reductive for specific occupations. Software developers needed a technical-stewardship anchor. Graphic designers needed a production-execution anchor. Paralegals needed a procedural-execution anchor. In each case, the calibration queue identified the structural gap; the fix was a richer function graph, not a new coefficient.

The pattern

Calibration identifies a structural miss. The fix goes into the model's own layers (formulas, function anchors, task-pressure rules). The external data stays outside the scoring loop. This keeps the runtime interpretable and prevents "score soup" where nobody can tell which input is driving the output.

What calibration sources are used

Three official external sources currently feed the calibration scaffold:

  • BLS Occupational Requirements Survey (ORS): The strongest official structural read on autonomy, supervision, and pace control. This is the main calibration source for the human-guardrail and accountability side of the model. Occupations without usable ORS rows are left unscored for that check until coverage improves.
  • Census ACS PUMS: American Community Survey public-use microdata. Provides an observable external read on within-occupation heterogeneity: wage dispersion, education dispersion, industry dispersion, and worker-mix spread. This is the main calibration source for the fragmentation and role-variant side of the model.
  • Census BTOS: Business Trends and Outlook Survey AI supplement. Provides an official external read on sector-level AI use, planned AI use, task-substitution intensity, and workflow-change intensity. This is the main calibration source for the adoption-context side of the model. Because these are business-use prevalence signals rather than task automability signals, the BTOS data is mapped into the model's organizational-conversion range before comparison.

Each of these sources is excellent for checking the model's assumptions. None of them is clean enough to trust as a direct scoring input without introducing more noise than signal. That is why they stay in the calibration layer.

Design Principles

These principles were not stated up front. They emerged from observing which design choices kept working and which ones kept breaking. They are descriptive, not aspirational: this is what the model actually does, not what it hopes to do.

  • Structure before prediction Represent what the role actually is before trying to predict what happens to it. If the structural model is wrong, no amount of evidence fixes the output. This is why the model spends so much effort on the task graph, function layer, and accountability profiles before it even starts scoring exposure. The representation has to be right first.
  • Move one layer lower When an abstraction is too coarse, do not add complexity at the same level. Move to a more granular layer. Occupation scores were too coarse, so the model moved to clusters. Clusters were too averaged, so it moved to tasks. Tasks alone missed authority and institutional structure, so it added functions. Each move solved a specific failure mode in the prior version.
  • Aggregate upward, never downward Public outputs should be derived from the most granular scored layer, not projected from a higher-level prior. Task rows drive cluster summaries. Cluster summaries drive wave timing. Wave timing drives role fate. If you project downward (from cluster to task), you are imposing assumptions. If you aggregate upward (from task to cluster), you are summarizing evidence.
  • Keep explicit fallbacks When task evidence is strong, use it. When it is sparse, fall back to cluster priors. When cluster evidence is weak, fall back to occupation priors. The system never silently trusts weak evidence; every fallback tier is labeled and visible in the output. This means the model's confidence varies by task, and that variation is a feature, not a bug.
  • External data calibrates, never enters runtime Every external dataset is excellent for checking assumptions. Very few are clean enough to trust as direct scoring inputs without introducing more noise than signal. Calibration changes the model indirectly by exposing where the model's own layers are structurally wrong. The fix always goes into the model's layers, not into the scoring formula as a direct external input.
  • Every score must be explainable per-task If a score cannot be traced back to specific task rows with source labels, evidence tiers, and causal notes, the model is hiding an average. Averages are comfortable because they are stable. They are also misleading because they conceal which tasks are driving the result and which are just noise. The explanation surface should always be auditable.
  • Friction is human advantage, not just difficulty Automation difficulty is not one number. It is a composite of intrinsic friction (0.40), human advantage (0.25), empirical resistance (0.25), and coupling protection (0.10). A task can be technically reachable but still retained because of judgment, trust, or organizational coupling. "Can AI do this?" and "Will a firm delegate this to AI?" are different questions with different answers, and the friction model tries to represent both.
  • Distinguish exposure from displacement This is the principle that underlies the entire project. Task exposure measures technological overlap. Role transformation measures organizational consequences. The gap between them is where most of the decision-relevant information lives. Any model that collapses that gap into a single score is answering a less useful question than it could be.
The central design pattern

Identify where the current abstraction is too coarse. Move one layer lower. Keep explicit fallback behavior. Aggregate upward only after lower-level scoring is coherent. That is the pattern behind every iteration in this system. The model was built by progressively replacing hidden averages with explicit structure.

Why the model evolved this way

The sequence was not arbitrary. Each iteration solved a concrete failure mode in the prior version:

  1. Occupation scores were too coarse.
  2. Cluster priors improved decomposition but were still too averaged.
  3. Richer task inventories made the role structurally legible.
  4. Dependency edges made spillover legible.
  5. Function anchors made retained human value legible.
  6. Editable composition made the model user-specific instead of occupation-generic.
  7. Task-level scoring made the explanation concrete.
  8. Task-evidence blending made the task layer responsive to empirical evidence.
  9. Task-derived public outputs removed a public/runtime mismatch.
  10. The task-source resolver made the evidence path less brittle and less proxy-dependent.
  11. The structural calibration scaffold kept external checks outside runtime instead of turning every new dataset into a score input.
  12. Reviewed role variants and supplemental anchors gave heterogeneous occupations richer starting points without forcing every occupation into explicit splits.

The direction of travel has been: occupation averages, cluster priors, task graph, function graph, task scoring, task-derived public outputs, source-resolved task evidence, non-runtime structural calibration, and reviewed structural anchors.

The remaining steps are: keep improving task-first scoring until strong task evidence is the default starting point whenever coverage is high enough, and review whether any calibration layer is strong enough to justify a narrow runtime promotion without collapsing interpretability.

That would be the cleanest next version of the model.
