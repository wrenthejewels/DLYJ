Before the Model: What the Field Already Knew

This model does not start from scratch. There is already a serious literature on automation, task exposure, complementarity, adoption, and labor-market adjustment. A rational reader should understand that lineage first, because the whole exercise is an attempt to cash out what that literature implies if you care about roles, not just task overlap.

The shortest version is this. Exposure scores were a real advance because they moved the conversation away from “whole occupations disappear at once” and toward “specific tasks become cheaper, faster, or delegable.” But the stronger economics that followed did not stop there. In the task-based tradition associated with authors like Autor, Acemoglu, and Restrepo, automation changes labor demand through several channels at once: displacement in some tasks, complementarity in others, and sometimes the creation or reinstatement of new work. That is the epistemic road that leads to the architecture on this site.

Exposure scores are useful They are a disciplined way to ask where AI can touch the task bundle. They are not a disciplined way to infer the whole employment outcome by themselves.
Task economics is better than occupation essentialism The field improved when it stopped asking “is this job automated?” and started asking which tasks are displaced, complemented, or newly created.
Complementarity is not a soft excuse It is an economic claim about where productivity gains still require human judgment, trust, authority, customer interface, or workflow ownership.
The evidence is early, not empty Macro unemployment has not broken sharply, but adoption is fast, productivity gains are real, and some exposed hiring channels already look softer than the background labor market.

The exposure-score literature matters because it disciplined the problem. GPTs are GPTs made the core point legible: large language models overlap with a surprisingly broad set of tasks, including many well-paid knowledge-work occupations. But a more mature reading of that result is not “therefore the jobs vanish.” It is “therefore the old one-number exposure framing is no longer enough.”

This is also where organizations like the IMF and the ILO sharpened the picture. The right question is no longer just “how exposed is this occupation?” It is “how exposed, how complementary, how adoptable, and how substitutable is the remaining human work?” That is exactly the gap this model is trying to close.

That last point is easy to miss in public discussion. Three things can be true at once without contradiction: current macro job loss is limited, AI productivity effects are already real, and some categories of work are still plausibly at genuine medium-term risk. The latest Anthropic labor-market work makes this especially clear: broad task exposure is visible, unemployment effects are not yet obvious, but young-worker hiring in exposed paths may already be softening — a pattern that Brynjolfsson, Chandar, and Chen (2025) find is already measurable, with workers aged 22–25 in high-exposure jobs showing a 6 percent employment drop between 2022 and 2025. Meanwhile, recent Acemoglu-framework analysis finds 84 occupations have now crossed a moderate displacement-risk threshold. That is exactly the kind of early-warning pattern a serious model should try to reason about.

That is the pre-model worldview this site is built on. Exposure research gives you the front door. Labor economics tells you why that front door does not settle the employment question. Recent adoption and productivity work tells you the issue is live right now, not hypothetical. The remaining job is to model the missing middle: how exposed work gets reorganized into a surviving, compressed, complemented, rebundled, or displaced role.

What This Model Is Trying to Answer

The core question here is not simply whether AI touches a role. It is what kind of thing that contact means. Most roles do not jump directly from “AI can do some tasks” to “the job is gone.” They pass through a chain of structural questions first.

The model is built around one hard separation: technical exposure, organizational conversion, and role survival are different questions. If you merge them too early, you get a clean-looking answer to the wrong problem.

That is the opening move of the whole guide. The point is not to make the model feel more complicated than it needs to be. The point is to keep different kinds of uncertainty from masquerading as one number. A technical claim about what AI can execute is not yet a claim about managerial adoption, and neither of those is yet a claim about whether the role still has a coherent human core after workflow changes land.

A lot of bad discourse in this area comes from skipping those separations. People move directly from capability demos to labor conclusions, or from broad productivity effects to confident predictions about which seats vanish. The model is designed to force slower reasoning than that. It asks what kind of evidence is being presented, what layer of the mechanism that evidence actually speaks to, and what would still have to be true before a role genuinely compresses or breaks.

That is why the model is constructed as a sequence of epistemic layers rather than one blended score. One layer asks where AI can perform the work. The next asks whether firms can actually reorganize around that. The next asks whether enough human-owned function remains for the seat to stay coherent. The defensible question is: how does a role transform as AI pressure rises, and under what conditions does that transformation become compression, rebundling, complement, or displacement?

Represent the role before predicting the role If the task mix or function map is wrong, later scoring layers are not enough to rescue the result.
Keep mechanism and audit separate External data is used to test whether the model looks plausible, not to hide every disagreement inside one blended score.
Forecast transformation before displacement The central question is what the seat becomes as AI pressure rises, not just whether it disappears.

Why Exposure Scores Alone Are Not Enough

Exposure scores are useful. They measure technological overlap between AI capabilities and work tasks. But people usually ask them to answer a much bigger question: what happens to the job? That is where exposure-only approaches break down.

The model on this site still uses exposure-like signals. It just refuses to treat them as fate. Direct task evidence is one layer in the stack, not the whole story.

The reason is straightforward. Jobs are not just bundles of executable steps. They are also bundles of ownership, escalation, trust, sequencing, sign-off, customer management, institutional friction, and economic incentive. Exposure scores are good at telling you where AI can touch the work. They are much worse at telling you what the firm will do with that fact, or what kind of human role remains once the easy-to-execute parts have been compressed.

This matters because the same exposure number can sit on top of several very different labor stories. Sometimes AI mainly acts as a complement and raises throughput. Sometimes it carves out bounded execution while leaving the seat intact. Sometimes it compresses support work around a still-human bottleneck. And sometimes it really does create genuine displacement pressure. If those paths all begin from “high exposure,” then exposure is an entry point, not a conclusion.

This is why the model does not just average task exposure to the occupation level and call it a forecast. That approach erases the things that actually determine whether the seat survives: task centrality, support-vs-core structure, spillover across connected work, and whether the remaining human-owned work still forms a coherent job.

Those differences are exactly why the model adds a dependency graph, a function layer, retained-human metrics, and a separate recomposition layer. Exposure scores are useful for locating pressure. They are not enough to explain what pressure does once it enters an organization — a point the Federal Reserve's research on AI adoption and job-posting behavior makes concrete: firms adopting AI show no reduction in job postings, modest headcount effects of around -4 percent, and productivity gains of +11.5 percent — exposure and outcome are not the same signal.

How the Role Gets Built

Before the model scores anything, it has to decide what role it is actually scoring. That sounds basic, but it is the first discipline in the whole build: do not forecast a role until you have represented the role.

That discipline is easy to underestimate. A lot of systems quietly assume the occupation title is already the role. But titles are usually too coarse for serious forecasting. The work done by a financial analyst inside a bank is not the same as the work done by a financial analyst inside a startup, and a title like “software developer” can hide radically different mixes of implementation, debugging, architecture, compliance, stakeholder translation, and operational ownership.

So the model starts by building an active composition rather than scoring a label. It assembles tasks, functions, dependencies, reviewed variants, and user edits into one structural object. That way later layers are working on a thing that actually resembles the seat, not an occupational average that becomes less plausible the moment the user looks at it closely.

This model builds a role from several layers at once: an occupation baseline, reviewed task additions, function anchors, dependency edges, role variants, and then any user edits. The goal is not to pretend one job title maps to one fixed bundle. The goal is to create a defensible starting composition, then let the user move it closer to the real seat they occupy.

The important methodological rule is simple: the user changes the structure, and the model re-scores the structure. That is more interpretable than pretending the role stays fixed while a few hidden coefficients move in the background.

Once you separate tasks, functions, and dependencies, later parts of the model become much more interpretable. You can ask not just “what is exposed?” but “what was central, what was support work, and what human-owned purpose still remains after the exposed work gets cheaper?”

How the Data Sources Work Together

The model uses about a dozen external data sources. That can look like a lot. The reason it needs all of them is that each one answers a fundamentally different question — and those questions cannot be collapsed into each other without destroying exactly the information you need.

The cleanest way to see this is to separate the four distinct roles that data sources play. Each role sits at a different layer of the problem, and they feed the model in a specific order.

Role construction: what are we actually scoring? Before the model can score anything, it needs to accurately represent the role. O*NET provides the baseline task inventory — 20–40 task statements per occupation, task frequencies, and a skill profile. But O*NET descriptions lag current practice by years. Whole categories of modern work are absent: managing Slack channels, triaging support tickets, running sprint retrospectives. Reviewed job-posting expansions fill those gaps, using current task language scraped from actual listings.

Tasks alone are still not enough structure. The model also needs a dependency graph — explicit edges describing which tasks enable, support, review, or feed other tasks. There is no external source for this. It was built by hand across 1,176 edges, because no database describes how work inside a role depends on other work inside the same role. Without this graph, pressure on one task looks like a contained event; with it, the model can trace how automating a data-gathering task devalues the coordination work that was built around it.

The function layer goes one more level up. Where tasks describe how work gets done, functions describe why the seat exists. A compliance officer's function of interpreting controls and making compliance decisions carries a delegability guardrail of around 0.78 — meaning institutional structure significantly prevents full delegation even when the underlying tasks are technically exposed. That profile was reviewed by hand, because O*NET task statements cannot tell you whether the role's organizational authority is actually transferable to AI.

Task evidence: what has AI demonstrated it can actually do? Once the role is built, the model needs to know what AI has demonstrated it can handle for each task. The Anthropic Economic Index is the primary empirical source: actual observed AI usage across task categories, derived from Claude.ai and API telemetry, classified against O*NET tasks. It is the closest available signal to real-world evidence about which tasks are actually landing in AI workflows today.

But task evidence varies in confidence. Some tasks have many observations; others have few. Rather than treating all evidence equally, the model uses a five-tier evidence stack: live AEI evidence first, then reviewed task estimates, then benchmark labels from external literature (GPTs are GPTs, the Brynjolfsson/Mitchell/Rock SML rubric, Webb, AIOE), then cluster priors, then occupation fallbacks. When evidence is thin, the model applies shrinkage — blending the task's own score toward its cluster prior rather than trusting a noisy estimate at face value.

A reviewed task estimate for a specific occupation can outrank a broader benchmark label, even when the benchmark is from an empirical paper. The reason is specificity. A benchmark says "tasks of this kind are generally exposed." A reviewed estimate says "this specific task, inside this occupation's function and authority structure, behaves like this." The narrower, more specific claim wins.

Labor context: how fast does capability convert into change? Technical capability and organizational adoption are different things. A task can be technically reachable by AI today and still not be compressed into AI workflow for years, because firms face implementation costs, liability structures, and change management friction. This layer measures not what AI can do, but how quickly that capability is converting into actual workflow change for this occupation.

BLS OEWS wages and occupational projections tell the model where bargaining leverage and labor demand are heading. BLS CPS data tracks unemployment tightness in the occupation group. Census BTOS data measures how much AI adoption is actually happening at the business level, broken out by sector. Since occupations concentrate in different industries, the model builds an occupation-specific adoption context from the BTOS sector mix.

One finding from this layer that changed how the model was built: individual AI usage and organizational adoption are not the same signal, and they can diverge sharply. Customer service representatives show 0.70 individual Claude usage but only 0.16 organizational AI adoption context. Software developers show the reverse: 0.29 individual usage, 0.85 organizational adoption. The first measures who is experimenting with AI tools; the second measures where organizations are restructuring workflows around them. Collapsing these into one number would mask exactly the distinction that matters.

External calibration: does the structural story hold up from outside? The core scoring layers can be internally consistent and still be structurally wrong in ways that only show up against independent observations. The calibration layer uses external sources to pressure-test the model's assumptions from outside — but it uses them differently than the sources above.

BLS ORS (Occupational Requirements Survey) is the strongest independent read on how much human autonomy, supervisory responsibility, and judgment an occupation actually requires. When ORS-backed review showed that the model was overweighting trust and regulatory liability relative to judgment and human authority in the retained accountability formula, the formula was adjusted — not by blending ORS data into the score, but by revising the formula itself.

Census ACS PUMS microdata provides a real external measure of within-occupation heterogeneity: wage dispersion, education dispersion, industry dispersion. When ACS showed that an occupation title covered structurally different roles, the model added reviewed role variants rather than forcing all variants through one flat baseline.

The rule that governs this is strict: calibration sources expose where the model's own layers are wrong. The fix always goes into the model's own layers — revised friction profiles, richer function anchors, explicit role variants — never into the scoring formula as a direct external input. The calibration stack diagnoses. The internal structure is what gets repaired.

The reason this separation matters is interpretability. When the model shows a structural state or a timing estimate, you should be able to trace back which layer produced it. Blending task exposure, firm adoption pace, labor trends, and external consistency checks into one composite number produces a precise-looking result you cannot interrogate. Keeping the layers separated is what makes the output auditable — and what makes it possible to find and fix the model's mistakes.

Decomposing Work into Clusters

The model decomposes each occupation into a weighted bundle of 12 task clusters, or task families. An occupation-level exposure score treats the role as an indivisible atom: it cannot show which parts of the work are under pressure, or explain why two financial analysts at different firms face different levels of risk despite sharing a job title. Decomposing to clusters makes that distinction possible.

Each cluster carries its own automation-difficulty estimate and its own timing-frontier read. Instead of saying "this occupation is 55% exposed," the model can say "documentation pressure arrives earlier, analysis pressure arrives later, and stakeholder-management work stays harder to absorb."

This was a pragmatic choice for several reasons:

  • Cluster priors are easier to review than task-level scores. With 12 clusters, a human reviewer can check each one. With hundreds of task rows, that gets harder.
  • Cluster priors are more stable than sparse task-level evidence. When you only have strong evidence for a few tasks, the cluster average gives you a workable fallback rather than noise.
  • Clusters make the timing model interpretable. Pressure starts in different parts of the role at different times. Clusters are the natural unit for "this work is under pressure sooner" versus "this work is under pressure later."

But clusters have a fundamental weakness: they are still averages. Tasks inside a cluster can differ materially in how exposed they are, how important they are to the role, and how much human leverage they carry. A cluster called "analysis and research" might contain both "gather publicly available data" (highly automatable) and "exercise judgment about ambiguous client situations" (not automatable in the same way). The cluster average papers over that distinction.

So cluster priors were a workable scaffold, not an end state. They solved the decomposition problem without solving the granularity problem.

Tasks as the Scoring Unit

Clusters group tasks by family, but the model needs to reason about individual tasks: their relative importance, their relationships to each other, and how much each one explains why the role exists.

O*NET provides task statements for every occupation, but they are not sufficient for many of the roles the model covers. Some occupations have only 20 task rows, thin enough that the cluster composition becomes fragile. Many current workflow tasks — managing Slack channels, triaging tickets, running retrospectives — are absent because O*NET descriptions lag actual practice by years.

The model expands the task layer with several additions:

  • Reviewed job-description task additions that filled gaps where O*NET underdescribed a role
  • Stable internal task IDs so a task could be tracked, edited, and scored consistently even when public sources described the same work differently
  • Value centrality: how much each task explains why the role exists. A task with high value centrality is one that, if removed, would call into question whether the seat is still needed.
  • Bargaining power weight: how much worker leverage depends on performing this task. Some tasks are important to the organization but do not give the worker much leverage. Others are the reason the worker is hard to replace.
  • Role criticality: whether the task is core, supporting, or optional to the role's structural identity.
  • AI support observability: whether the task's AI-assisted output is distinguishable from fully human output, which affects how much organizational trust the task retains.

Once tasks carry their own attributes, the model can ask a more useful question than "how exposed is this occupation?": "Which specific tasks explain why this role exists, and which of those tasks are reachable by AI?"

That is a different question with a different answer. An occupation can be 60% exposed at the task level and still structurally sound if the exposed tasks are all supporting work and the core tasks that define the role are in the distant wave. Conversely, an occupation that is only 30% exposed can be structurally fragile if the exposed tasks happen to be the ones that explain why the seat exists.

Task-level attributes made that distinction visible.

The Dependency Graph

Tasks are the nodes. The dependency graph provides the edges.

Real work is not a flat list of independent activities. Tasks depend on each other: some exist mainly to enable other tasks, some review the output of other tasks, some only have value because upstream work produces the input they consume.

The model added a dependency graph with 1,176 explicit edges across the 61 selected occupations. Each edge connects a source task to a target task with a dependency type (supports, reviews, enables, feeds, follows_from) and a strength weight.

This introduced one of the most important conceptual upgrades in the whole system: the distinction between direct pressure and indirect pressure.

  • Direct pressure means AI can perform the task itself. The task is technologically reachable.
  • Indirect pressure means the task becomes less necessary because adjacent work changes. The task may not be automatable at all, but it loses value when the upstream work it supports is compressed or eliminated.

This distinction matters enormously for support work. Consider an office coordinator whose primary tasks include routing information between teams, scheduling follow-up meetings based on project updates, and maintaining status trackers. None of those tasks are particularly easy to automate directly. They require context, judgment about priorities, and organizational knowledge.

But if the upstream project work is restructured by AI, if status updates are generated automatically, if meeting coordination is handled by scheduling tools, then the coordinator's tasks do not get "automated" in the naive sense. They get devalued. The work that made the coordinator's tasks necessary has shifted, and the support tasks lose their structural reason to exist.

Without the dependency graph, the model would score that coordinator's tasks independently and conclude that they are resistant to automation. With the graph, it can trace the indirect pressure from upstream changes and arrive at a more accurate assessment.

The reverse is also true: some tasks gain value when adjacent tasks are exposed. If AI handles routine data preparation, the analyst's interpretation and judgment tasks become the binding constraint, and their value increases. The graph captures both directions.

The Function Layer

The task graph captures what the work is and how pressure moves between tasks. What it does not capture is why the role exists.

Tasks describe how work gets done. They do not describe what the human role is there to own.

That distinction is concrete. "AI can write first drafts of legal briefs" is a statement about task exposure. "The legal system still requires a human attorney to own the representation" is a statement about institutional structure, accountability, and delegation authority. They live in different layers of the problem.

What functions represent

The model added a function layer on top of the task graph. A function is not a task. It is the durable organizational purpose that a bundle of tasks supports. Functions answer the question: if all the exposed tasks got cheaper tomorrow, would a coherent human-owned responsibility still remain?

Some examples from the model's actual function data:

  • A compliance officer's primary function is compliance interpretation and control assurance. The accountability profile carries judgment of 0.84, trust of 0.80, regulatory liability of 0.78, and human authority of 0.70. Many of the tasks under that function are exposed (document review, report generation, checklist verification). But the function of interpreting controls and making compliance decisions cannot be delegated to AI by institutional definition. The role recomposes; it does not collapse.
  • A software developer's primary function is software delivery: translating product and system needs into working software. The primary accountability profile carries judgment of 0.80, trust of 0.60, and human authority of 0.58. The developer also has a supplemental system-reliability function for production behavior, review quality, and technical integrity (judgment 0.80, human authority 0.62). Routine coding tasks are highly exposed. But the stewardship function survives because someone still needs to own technical decisions that shape the system's long-term trajectory.
  • A paralegal's primary function is procedural reliability: matter-ready case support under attorney direction. The primary accountability profile carries judgment of 0.72, trust of 0.78, regulatory liability of 0.74, and human authority of 0.54. The paralegal also has a supplemental matter-coordination function with human authority of 0.46. The task exposure is high; the retained function is real but supervised. The role compresses toward the coordination work, not toward elimination.
  • A surgeon's primary function is operative patient care: direct physical intervention under intraoperative conditions requiring real-time judgment, manual precision, and full clinical accountability. The accountability profile carries judgment of 0.92, human authority of 0.94, trust of 0.88, and regulatory liability of 0.90. This is near the minimum delegability end of the distribution — institutional, legal, and patient-safety constraints make AI handoff of the core function structurally impossible under current frameworks, regardless of AI capability. The role is exposed on peripherals (notes, scheduling, pre-op synthesis) but the function center is heavily anchored.
  • A data entry operator's primary function is accurate transcription: moving structured information between systems according to predefined rules. The accountability profile carries judgment of 0.14, human authority of 0.18, trust of 0.22, and regulatory liability of 0.06. This is near the maximum delegability end — the function is defined entirely by rules, not by interpretation, and organizational friction against handoff is low. The seat compresses rapidly as substitution improves accuracy and speed. This is the case where task exposure and seat outcome do directly correspond, because there is no function layer cushioning the transition.

Why a task-only model gets this wrong

A task-only model would look at a paralegal with 60% of tasks exposed and conclude the role is under severe displacement pressure. The function layer adds crucial context: yes, the execution tasks are exposed, but the legal-support function that justifies the seat is retained under attorney direction, and institutional structure (bar rules, court procedures, liability allocation) prevents full delegation regardless of AI capability.

The same logic applies to accountants, financial analysts, compliance officers, and many professional roles. The tasks are exposed. The functions are retained. The role changes shape but does not disappear.

Without the function layer, the model would systematically overstate displacement risk for professional and judgment-heavy roles. With it, the model can distinguish between "the work gets cheaper" and "the seat goes away."

The accountability dimensions

Each function in the model carries an explicit accountability profile with five dimensions:

  • Judgment requirement (0 to 1): How much interpretive discretion, contextual reasoning, or tradeoff decision-making the function demands.
  • Human authority requirement (0 to 1): Whether someone in the role must hold formal decision-making power or sign-off authority.
  • Trust requirement (0 to 1): Whether the work depends on stakeholder, client, or institutional trust in a specific human. This is not "trust that the person is competent." It is about whether the institutional relationship requires a named human accountable party.
  • Regulatory liability weight (0 to 1): Whether the role carries legal, compliance, or institutional liability for decisions.
  • Delegability guardrail (computed): The institutional friction against simply assigning the function to AI. This is the composite signal that captures whether judgment, authority, trust, or liability structurally prevent full delegation, regardless of AI capability.

These dimensions are not abstract quality scores. They are structural properties of the role's position in an organization. A high delegability guardrail means the function cannot be handed off to AI because of institutional, legal, or organizational constraints, not because the AI is not good enough yet.

How functions change the model's conclusions

The function layer flows into the model through several paths:

Retained function strength measures how much of the role's human-owned purpose survives after task exposure. The formula weights delegability guardrails at 42% of the signal, because institutional structure is the most durable source of human retention. A role with high delegability guardrails retains function strength even when most of its tasks are exposed.

Retained accountability strength is a six-term formula combining delegability (20%), human authority (22%), judgment (18%), unexposed share (18%), trust (12%), and regulatory liability (10%). This measures whether the role still carries real human decision ownership, not just residual tasks.

Coupling protection flows into the timing and difficulty calculation. Functions add friction to automation difficulty through the formula's coupling term (weighted at 10%). Higher-function roles are harder to fully automate because the role's structural obligations create friction even when task-level work is exposed.

These signals now feed directly into two higher-level reads. The canonical trajectory layer asks how execution compression, demand response, and structural necessity interact over time. The structural-state layer then asks what state the role actually reads as over time: retained, complemented, compressed, rebundled, or displaced. Without the function layer, the model would mostly have task exposure and timing pressure to work with, and it would systematically misclassify professional roles as facing worse outcomes than they actually face.

A Worked Example: Financial Analyst

The concepts above are easiest to follow when traced through a single role. Here is how the model processes a financial analyst at a mid-size firm — from role builder through evidence resolution, pressure propagation, and final state read.

Step 1: Role builder. The model starts with O*NET's task rows for Financial Analysts (SOC 13-2051), which gives roughly 25 tasks. The reviewed layer adds work common in practice but absent from O*NET — running variance analysis in planning tools, managing ad-hoc data requests from business partners, maintaining model templates across planning cycles. Function anchors attach three clusters: a drafting and documentation cluster, a data synthesis and analysis cluster, and a decision support cluster. The function layer scores moderate judgment requirement in decision support (0.62), low-to-moderate human authority (the analyst advises, rarely signs), and moderate trust requirement (the analyst owns the interpretation but is not the accountability holder). Delegability guardrails land mid-range: not a fiduciary or compliance-bearing seat, but not trivially handing off judgment either.

Step 2: Evidence resolution. Moving down the evidence ladder, the tasks divide into three groups. Drafting tasks — variance commentary, narrative packaging, preliminary synthesis — have direct Anthropic Economic Index coverage showing high and growing adoption. These land at rung 1 (live task evidence) with high confidence. Budget scenario modeling and sensitivity analysis fall to rung 2 (reviewed task estimates), because the task-level judgment involved is nuanced enough that the AEI coverage alone doesn't fully resolve it. Stakeholder communication and relationship management fall to rung 4 (cluster prior), where there is limited direct evidence for this exact combination of task and occupation. The model labels each row's source tier, so the analyst can see exactly which scores rest on direct evidence versus inherited fallback.

Step 3: Pressure propagation. Direct pressure enters hardest at the drafting and data-pull clusters. Those clusters show high automation difficulty within the near-term wave — narrative drafting and data synthesis are among the earliest tasks to face real substitution. The variance analysis cluster absorbs inherited pressure from data-pull through a moderately weighted dependency edge (roughly 0.20). The decision-support cluster inherits weaker indirect pressure from the drafting cluster (roughly 0.14) — there is less for the analyst to synthesize at the interpretive layer because the earlier synthesis step is already moving to tools. The stakeholder and sign-off clusters are largely sheltered from direct pressure, but the dependency edges still carry a weak signal into them. The overall picture: roughly 45–55% of the role's task weight faces near-term or next-wave pressure, with the retained core anchored in the decision-support and stakeholder functions.

Step 4: Outcome. The financial analyst role does not break because the function layer holds. The decision-support cluster carries enough judgment requirement that the delegability guardrail prevents clean handoff. At the year-2 checkpoint, transformed share is rising but retained accountability is still strong — the model reads the role as complemented: the analyst does more, faster, with AI handling the drafting load. By the year-5 checkpoint, transformed share reaches around 0.55 and scope starts to narrow. The role is entering compression on the execution side but remains coherent because the human-owned interpretive function is still structurally necessary. The year-10 read resolves as compressed or rebundled rather than displaced — the seat survives in a narrower form, focused on higher-judgment synthesis, stakeholder translation, and decision ownership. The primary trigger is delegate, leading to a complement then compress trajectory rather than a structural break.

How the Evidence Hierarchy Works

Not all evidence about AI and work is making the same claim. Some sources observe a specific task directly. Some offer a narrower reviewed estimate. Some only provide a broad family-level fallback. The model has to keep those claims in order instead of averaging them into false precision.

The engine builds its initial score from the cluster level — broad groupings of task types that share automation difficulty characteristics. Cluster priors are the scaffold: stable fallback estimates that keep the model grounded when task-level evidence is absent. But they are not the final word. As soon as task-level evidence is available — from direct observation, reviewed estimates, or benchmark labels — it takes over. The cluster prior becomes the floor, not the ceiling. This shift from cluster scaffold to task-level evidence is one of the model's most important structural choices, because it means precision improves as evidence accumulates rather than staying permanently at the coarse-bucket level.

This is one of the strongest epistemic commitments in the model. It is not enough to have “more data.” The data has to be interpreted according to what kind of claim it is allowed to make. Direct task evidence is narrow and concrete. A reviewed estimate can be narrower than a benchmark label if it actually speaks to the exact row being scored. Cluster priors are useful, but only as inherited structure when better evidence is absent.

The hierarchy exists to stop the model from laundering uncertainty into apparent certainty. If the engine does not know the exact task well, it should be allowed to borrow from broader structure. But it should also have to admit that it is borrowing. That is why provenance matters so much in the runtime: the model is not just scoring tasks, it is also exposing how much of that score comes from direct observation, deliberate review, or fallback inheritance.

The key rule is specificity. A source that says something about this exact task row should usually outrank a source that only says something about tasks of that general kind. But the model also has to decide how much to trust that source, which is why reliability and fallback are separate steps.

Dependency graphs are hand-built
The cluster edges and weights represent reviewed structural judgments. They are not derived from empirical observation of actual workflow data. A role with an unusual cluster structure may not fit the reviewed graph well.
LIMIT 2
Cluster priors are averages
When the model falls back to cluster priors, it is borrowing from a family-level average that may not describe your specific role variant. Roles that differ significantly from the occupation baseline inherit this imprecision at the cluster level.
LIMIT 3
Demand response is estimated
Whether productivity gains expand or contract demand for a role is one of the hardest empirical questions in labor economics. The model uses a calibrated estimate, not a measured value. The trajectory at year 5 and year 10 should be treated as a structural read, not a prediction.

That ordering still does not mean the model trusts every source equally. Reliability is a separate question. A thin direct observation should not be treated like dense task evidence, and a broad prior should not pretend to be row-level knowledge.

That is also why the model is not a black-box predictor. Every task row carries a source tier and supporting evidence trail. When the model leans on fallback structure, it says so. When it uses direct evidence, it says that too.

How Pressure Spreads Through the Role

Once the model has a role structure and a best-available evidence read for each task, it still has not answered the main question. It only knows where direct AI pressure starts. The next step is to model how that pressure moves through the role.

This is where a flat task list stops being enough. Work inside a role is connected. Some tasks support other tasks. Some tasks only exist because a core function exists. Some tasks can shrink when adjacent work gets delegated to tools, even if the task itself is not the main site of direct AI exposure. And some functions remain sticky because they own escalation, judgment, or final accountability even after surrounding work becomes easier or thinner.

That is why the model uses graph logic. The graph is not ornamental complexity. It is the mechanism that lets one exposed task create spillover while still allowing the role to retain a human-owned center of gravity. Without that layer, the model would systematically miss compression, overstate independence between tasks, and confuse localized execution pressure with full-role collapse.

That matters because work is not organized as isolated rows. Some tasks are central. Some only exist because another task exists. Some tasks get squeezed when adjacent work gets cheaper. Others become more important because they are the remaining human bottleneck. The model needs a graph and a function layer to tell those stories apart.

The graph propagates pressure at the cluster level. Each cluster carries a baseline automation difficulty derived from its task mix. Edges connect clusters with numeric weights representing how much of one cluster's downstream activity depends on another. When a source cluster faces direct pressure, the adjacent clusters absorb a share of that pressure proportional to the edge weight, even if their own tasks are not directly exposed.

A concrete example: the drafting cluster sits upstream of the QA and review cluster in most professional roles. If AI takes over first-draft production in the drafting cluster, the review workload built around those drafts does not disappear immediately, but it loses volume. The review cluster faces inherited pressure through the edge because there is less to review, not because review tasks are themselves automatable in the same way. The model tracks this separately as indirect dependency pressure and weights it differently from the direct task signal.

The edge weights also attenuate the signal. A drafting-to-review edge carries roughly 18 percent of drafting pressure into the review cluster. A drafting-to-decision-support edge carries roughly 14 percent. This prevents one exposed corner from appearing to collapse the whole role, while still registering that real downstream exposure exists. Tasks far from the original source inherit weaker signals than tasks directly adjacent to it.

That single-task view is useful, but the model really becomes interpretable when you see pressure spread across connected tasks and functions rather than staying frozen on one row.

That is why the model does not ask only “how much of the role is exposed?” It asks where the pressure starts, where it spreads, what it compresses, and what still anchors human ownership after the workflow changes.

How the Continuous Forecast Works

The live model still exports simple timing buckets like current, next, and distant, but those are only summaries. The actual engine underneath them is continuous.

That matters because job transformation is rarely a clean threshold event. Roles change shape over time. Some start with noticeable present-day workflow pressure but retain integrity for years. Some look stable at first and then tip once adoption, delegation, and recomposition reinforce one another. Some move toward complement rather than compression. A coarse bucket can summarize those paths, but it should not be the thing generating them.

The continuous layer is trying to represent that shape directly. It follows transformed share, retained integrity, and state checkpoints across time, then derives simpler labels afterward for readability. That order matters. When the simpler label comes first, it flattens real differences. When the continuous path comes first, the bucket becomes what it should be: a coarse way of talking about a more detailed forecast.

The runtime now follows two linked curves over time. The first is a trajectory layer: how much of the role's work gets transformed as execution gets cheaper, adoption rises, and demand reacts. The second is a state layer: whether the role still reads as retained, complemented, compressed, rebundled, or displaced as those changes accumulate.

That continuous structure lets the model say something much more useful than “this looks near-term.” It can ask what happens by year 2, what changes by year 5, and whether the role's state actually flips or merely recomposes as AI adoption spreads.

The main continuous variable the model tracks is transformed share: the fraction of the role's work that has moved from human execution toward AI-assisted or AI-delegated workflow. A role with transformed share of 0.10 still looks and functions like a human role. A role with transformed share of 0.65 has structurally changed even if it still exists on the org chart. The state labels — retained, complemented, compressed, rebundled, displaced — are interpretations of where transformed share lands, not inputs to it.

Each of the four checkpoints asks a different structural question. Year zero is the baseline: given today's adoption rates, task costs, and organizational friction, how much of the role is already moving? Year two tests whether adoption pressure and tool embedding are accelerating the trajectory or plateauing. Year five is the compression test: have enough adjacent tasks been transformed to start narrowing the role's scope? Year ten is the long-run read: does the role still hold together as a coherent human seat, does it recompose into something narrower, or does transformed share push past the structural threshold where the seat breaks?

The result is a model that can give both kinds of answer: a simple readability layer for users who want a quick summary, and a continuous, auditable path for anyone who wants to see how the role changes over time and why.

How the Model Chooses Assist, Delegate, Compress, or Break

The model does not jump straight from pressure to a fate label. First it asks what kind of transition the role appears to be entering. Is AI mainly helping the worker, taking over bounded execution, narrowing the seat, or pushing the role toward a structural break?

This layer exists because “AI is entering the workflow” is still too vague. The same pressure can be absorbed in very different ways. In some roles it mostly acts as assistive tooling. In others it produces bounded delegation while the human remains the clear owner. In others it narrows the job enough that headcount or scope starts to compress. And in the harshest cases it weakens the role's center of gravity so much that the seat begins to break structurally.

The trigger map is intentionally coarser than the full state forecast, but it still matters because it identifies the first mode of change. That first mode shapes how the rest of the trajectory should be read. A role entering an assist path is telling a different story from a role entering a compression path, even if both eventually converge on similar transformed-share numbers.

That trigger layer exists because “AI matters here” is still underspecified. A role can be heavily affected while remaining human-led. Another can face only moderate technical pressure yet still compress if the exposed work is central, observable, and easy to reorganize around. The trigger logic is the model's first answer to what kind of change starts here?

Inside the runtime, that map is made more concrete by a timing frontier. The frontier asks when different transition thresholds first clear under the role's current structure. Assist and delegate tell you where AI becomes meaningfully embedded in the workflow. Compress and structural break tell you where the seat itself starts to narrow or fail.

This is why trigger labels are useful but still limited. They tell you what kind of transition pressure the role faces first. They do not yet tell you what the role becomes after that pressure plays out over time.

Why Trigger Outcomes and State Outcomes Are Different

One of the easiest mistakes in this space is to confuse the first transition with the final state. A role can enter the delegate path and still remain complemented. A role can show compress pressure and still end up rebundled rather than displaced. The trigger is not the destination.

This distinction is what keeps the model from sounding more certain than it should. A trigger says which boundary becomes active first. A state says what the role looks like after that pressure has propagated through the rest of the system. Those are related claims, but they operate on different timescales and answer different questions.

If that distinction disappears, the model starts to overread its own signals. A near-term delegation trigger can be mistaken for eventual collapse, or a compression trigger can be mistaken for immediate disappearance. The state layer is there to resist that kind of over-interpretation by asking what human-owned function remains once the trigger has had time to work through the role.

This distinction matters because the model is trying to explain role shape, not just role shock. The trigger layer says what kind of mechanism turns on. The state layer says how the seat reads after the mechanism has had time to work through the role, the workflow, and the organization.

Each trigger outcome describes the first mode of structural change. Assist means AI enters the workflow as tooling: it speeds up execution, reduces research time, or generates first drafts, but the human still owns the full task from start to sign-off. Transformed share rises, but retained accountability stays high. Delegate means AI takes over bounded execution clusters: the human manages outputs, reviews decisions, and handles exceptions, but stops doing the underlying work itself. The seat survives but changes shape. Compress means the role narrows: exposed work constitutes enough of the seat that its removal reduces headcount or scope, not just workflow. Structural break means the retained human shell is too thin to anchor a coherent seat — the function exists but not as a distinct role.

The state outcomes answer a later question: what does the seat look like after the trigger mechanism has had time to propagate? Retained means the role changes little — a lower-pressure trigger, strong friction, retained function holds. Complemented means the role expands or holds by absorbing AI output: the human does more, faster, with AI handling production load. Compressed means scope or headcount narrows as the exposed share removes work without substituting new work for the remaining seat. Rebundled means the role survives in a reshaped form — some tasks disappear, new ones emerge or absorb from adjacent clusters. Displaced means the seat loses structural coherence: the remaining human function is too thin or too delegable to justify a distinct position.

The reason this distinction matters is that many public claims about AI and work skip directly from exposure to loss. The runtime here does not. It lets the role be exposed, tool-heavy, and even partially compressed while still concluding that the seat remains coherent in a complemented or rebundled form.

That is the distinction worth carrying into the rest of the site. The model can say “AI enters here” without claiming “the job dies here.” Triggers tell you what starts. States tell you what the role becomes.

Personalization and Evidence Quality

The task graph, dependency edges, function layer, and accountability profiles describe the structure of a role. Two further concerns sit on top of that structure: personalization and evidence quality.

Editable role composition

Real users do not match generic occupation bundles. A software developer at a startup does different work than a software developer at a bank. An accountant focused on audit work has a structurally different role from an accountant focused on financial reporting. The model needed to let users edit the role structure itself, not just answer a few questions about their situation.

The editable composition layer added:

  • Source-bucketed tasks you can add or remove
  • Editable function anchors that change which functions the role is scored against
  • Optional user-declared support links between tasks
  • Optional task-to-function link overrides
  • Reviewed role variants for the most heterogeneous occupations (currently 7), so the model can recommend a starting baseline that better matches your actual work

This is a more transparent way to personalize than nudging a few top-level coefficients. The user changes the structure; the model re-scores the structure. That is a much more direct interaction than "answer five questions and we will adjust your number."

The evidence problem

The scoring engine starts from cluster-level difficulty estimates projected onto individual tasks — a reasonable fallback when task-level evidence is thin, but not the intended end state. The five-tier evidence resolver lets the model use task-level evidence when it is strong, and fall back to cluster priors when it is not:

  1. Live task evidence from the Anthropic Economic Index (actual observed AI usage; the March 2026 report finds 49% of jobs have seen at least 25% of their tasks performed using Claude)
  2. Reviewed task estimates from manual expert review
  3. Benchmark task labels from published research (GPTs are GPTs, SML, etc.)
  4. Cluster prior proxies that attach cluster-level estimates to tasks
  5. Fallback task proxies when nothing better is available

The resolver tries each tier in order and uses the best available evidence. The model's confidence varies by task: some tasks are scored with direct empirical evidence, others with reviewed estimates, and others with cluster fallbacks. The source tier is labeled on every task row, so you (and any auditor) can see exactly where each score comes from.

The design principle here: weak evidence clearly labeled is better than strong-looking evidence from a hidden average. If a task's score comes from a cluster prior fallback, the model says so. It does not pretend to have task-level precision it does not have.

This is also why the model does not simply use AEI task scores as the primary input. Direct Anthropic task evidence covers all 61 selected occupations, with variable confidence per task and meaningful divergences on many occupation-task rows when compared against the model's own scoring. The five-tier stack lets Anthropic evidence dominate when it is strong and specific, while still preserving reviewed estimates, benchmark labels, and cluster priors as explicit backstops where the task-level evidence is thinner or more ambiguous.

Why reviewed estimates outrank proxies

A reviewed task estimate and a fallback proxy can both involve manual work, but they are not the same kind of claim. A reviewed task estimate says: "I looked at this exact task, in this exact occupation, and made a deliberate task-level judgment." A proxy says: "I do not know this exact task well enough, so I am borrowing from broader structure" (usually the task cluster, occupation prior, or a similar-task fallback).

That is why reviewed task estimates rank above fallback proxies. They are narrower, more specific, and closer to the unit the model actually scores. Proxies are what keep the system stable when evidence is sparse, but they are still inherited structure rather than task-specific knowledge.

The same logic explains why a reviewed task estimate can outrank a benchmark task label, even when the benchmark source is empirical. A benchmark label such as GPTs are GPTs is still often broader than the exact task row and occupation context in this model. It may say that tasks of this kind are generally exposed, but it may not capture how that task behaves inside a particular occupation's function layer, support graph, or sign-off structure. A good reviewed estimate can therefore be the better fit for the exact row the engine is scoring.

So the resolver is not saying "my judgment is better because it is mine." It is saying: prefer the source that makes the strongest claim about the exact task row being scored. First use live task evidence when it exists. If not, use a reviewed task-specific estimate. If not, use a benchmark task label. If not, inherit from broader structure. That is how the model tries to stay both empirically grounded and transparent about where it is still leaning on fallback structure.

Why the model is not a black-box predictor

A simpler approach would be a black-box predictor that takes occupation features and benchmark signals and produces one final risk number. That approach answers the explanatory question badly. The goal was a model where you can point to the task rows, the function map, the dependency path, and the evidence tier and understand why the output moved.

That choice has a cost. The system is more complex than a leaderboard. Each layer is carrying a different part of the mechanism, and that is the right kind of complexity for the problem.

Where It Stands Now

By March 2026, the model had stopped lacking whole conceptual layers. The work now is to make the existing layers more empirically grounded, less coarse, and easier to audit.

The model now works as an upward-aggregating pipeline:

  1. Resolve the occupation from the modeled occupation set.
  2. Build an editable task and function graph from reviewed structural data.
  3. Start from cluster-level difficulty as the stable fallback baseline.
  4. Project that baseline onto tasks so tasks become the active scoring unit.
  5. Resolve task-level evidence from the five-tier evidence stack.
  6. Let reliable task evidence alter the task score rather than hiding behind the cluster average.
  7. Propagate dependency spillover across the graph.
  8. Compute retained leverage and function retention after pressure moves.
  9. Aggregate tasks back upward into cluster summaries, timing-frontier reads, trajectory signals, and structural-state signals.
  10. Map those signals into user-facing outputs such as the transformed-share timeline, the occupation-state forecast, and the compatibility-layer fate label.

Why the page now leads with structural state

Fate labels, timing buckets, and transformation diagnostics can be useful, but they still push too much interpretation through the question "what label does this role get?" The better first question is: what is happening to the seat over time?

That shift produced the structural-state layer. Instead of starting from a label, the model now asks whether the retained role stays broad or narrows, whether one bottleneck starts carrying too much of the seat, whether exposed execution work is offset by stronger retained human work, and whether firms have enough incentive to keep pushing automation once the next bottleneck clears. That state path became the better top-level summary because it describes the role's shape, not just its headline outcome.

Why year 0 no longer starts near zero

One recurring failure mode was a transformation curve that started too close to zero even when present-day AI pressure was already obvious in knowledge-work roles. That was too detached from actual workflow change.

The fix was to add a present-day floor inside the transformation path. If a task already has high direct pressure, high observability, and supportive absorption conditions, the curve can start materially above zero. For occupations where observed individual AI usage exists, that observed usage now acts as a soft year-0 anchor. It does not replace task evidence, and it does not rewrite the rest of the decade. It only stops the model from pretending the present starts from an almost untouched baseline when that is no longer credible.

Why curve families were added

Another issue was that too many roles were producing similar chart shapes even when their underlying structures were different. The model could distinguish them internally, but the top chart was not showing enough of that difference.

The solution was to detect a small set of tipping points on the baseline state timeline and use them to assign a curve family such as stable hold, complement then hold, rebundle then hold, early compression, compression then break, late cliff, or demand expansion. That let the forecast surface show more role-specific paths instead of flattening different mechanisms into one generic silhouette.

Why the controls split in two

The role forecast at the top and the occupation landscape lower on the page answer different questions. The top section is about your scored role, including your edits, hierarchy, and questionnaire profile. The lower section is about comparing occupations on one shared baseline.

Keeping those on one shared control surface made the occupation comparison harder to interpret, because one person's edited role could distort the whole map. The controls are now split so the top section stays personal while the occupation landscape stays comparable across occupations.

Current scope

The modeled set currently covers 61 occupations. The reviewed posting-backed task layer contains 520 rows, with most occupations carrying 8 reviewed rows and smaller subsets carrying 12 or 16. The function layer contains 124 reviewed function rows, and every selected occupation now starts from a reviewed multi-anchor default function graph rather than a single flat anchor.

Recent Belief Updates

Three recent updates materially changed the model rather than merely maintaining it.

  • Drafting and documentation were too easy in the model. AEI-backed review showed the friction profiles for those clusters were understating judgment and tacit context. The right response was to change the profiles rather than explain the miss away.
  • Tacit knowledge and judgment mattered more than the prior weighting implied. External labor evidence made accountability look too dominant in the difficulty formula. The weights were revised to raise tacit context and judgment relative to accountability.
  • Firm adoption and individual usage were not the same signal. BTOS-derived adoption context and occupation-level AI usage could diverge in either direction. Customer Service Representatives show 0.70 individual Claude usage but only 0.16 organizational adoption context; Software Developers show the reverse (0.29 individual vs. 0.85 organizational). Those two numbers measure fundamentally different things: one reflects who is experimenting with AI tools, the other reflects where organizations are restructuring workflows around them. They stay separate rather than being forced into one blended story.

Why Calibration Stays Outside the Core Score

Calibration answers a different kind of question than scoring does. The core model asks what the role is made of, how exposed its tasks are, what functions remain human-owned, and how that structure changes over time. Calibration asks whether those internal judgments still look plausible when they meet outside evidence.

That separation is deliberate. Some data is strong enough to challenge the model without being the right thing to score directly. The clean split is:

  • Core scoring uses task, function, and evidence layers directly tied to the role model. These are the signals that describe what the role is, how exposed its tasks are, and what human-owned functions remain.
  • Calibration uses external context to pressure-test those layers. These are the signals that tell you whether the model's structural assumptions look plausible against independent observations.

The separation matters because each layer measures something different. Task automability is not the same thing as organizational adoption. Organizational adoption is not the same thing as labor-market demand. Labor-market demand is not the same thing as occupational quality. Blend them all into one score and the result stops being legible — which is the core finding of the Yale Budget Lab's review of AI exposure methodology, and what the ILO's analysis of AI exposure indicators makes explicit: single-score approaches systematically conflate structural claims that belong in separate layers. A high score could mean exposed tasks, aggressive firm adoption, weak bargaining power, or plain secular decline. Those are different claims and they should stay different.

This is one of the places where the model is trying hardest not to cheat. It would be easy to make the final score look more “realistic” by pushing outside labor data directly into it. But that would blur the boundary between mechanism and audit. The user would get a cleaner-looking number, but it would be harder to tell whether that number was being driven by task evidence, occupational structure, business adoption, or macro labor trends.

So calibration stays outside the main score on purpose. It is there to pressure-test the model's internal claims, not to hide those claims under a composite index. That keeps the runtime more interpretable and makes it possible to say not just that the model disagrees with an outside source, but exactly which internal layer is responsible for the disagreement.

The pattern: use outside data to expose model errors, fix at the correct layer

Calibration is not passive. It changes the model by exposing where a structural assumption is wrong. But the correction always lands inside the model's own layers, not as a direct external override inside the scoring formula.

The clearest examples are:

  • ORS for accountability. The BLS Occupational Requirements Survey provides the strongest outside read on autonomy, supervisory responsibility, and pace control. It exposed that retained accountability was leaning too heavily on trust and liability. The response was to reweight the internal formula toward delegability guardrails, human authority, and judgment. ORS identified the miss; it did not become the score.
  • BTOS for adoption. Census BTOS gives a business-side read on where organizations are actually deploying AI. That signal belongs in adoption, conversion, and timing context, not in task automability. The model uses it there and leaves the task layer alone.
  • ACS for heterogeneity. Census ACS PUMS gives a real read on how wide an occupation's internal spread is across wages, education, and industry mix. When that spread is large, one flat default role is usually too blunt. The fix is richer role variants and deeper function graphs, not an ACS coefficient stapled onto the final score.

How the Calibration Loop Works

The calibration loop is the model's outer error-correction layer. It compares live outputs against independent observations, identifies where the mismatch comes from, and sends the correction back to the internal layer that generated the bad assumption. The external data stays outside the runtime score. Its job is to falsify weak structure, not to blur the score into a composite index.

The cycle below makes that boundary concrete: each source answers a different question, each flagged mismatch points to a different model layer, and each fix has to survive a rerun without breaking the rest of the system.

That loop is what turns the model from a static theory into a maintainable one. Without it, every structural assumption would slowly drift toward intuition and inertia. With it, the model is forced to keep meeting outside reality: wage structure, supervisory requirements, business adoption, worker-side usage, occupational spread, and projected labor-market direction.

Just as important, the loop provides a disciplined way to change the model. A mismatch is not permission to improvise. It is a prompt to ask what claim failed, which layer made that claim, and whether a targeted correction improves the flagged area without damaging the rest of the runtime. That is the standard the calibration layer is trying to impose.

Why calibration exists at all

The model scores tasks using evidence, friction profiles, function anchors, and user responses. Those layers can be internally coherent and still be structurally wrong. A role can look well protected in the model while BLS survey data suggests weaker human authority. A role can look uniform in the default baseline while Census data shows large within-title spread across employers and industries.

Calibration exists to catch those misses. It corrects them by updating the model's own layers: revising a friction profile, adjusting a function anchor, adding a role variant, or correcting adoption and demand context. The rule is simple: outside data can challenge the model anywhere, but fixes must land where the model actually made the wrong claim.

What each external source measures

The calibration loop draws on seven external sources. Each one answers a different question about the occupation. The questions do not overlap cleanly, which is exactly why they should not be collapsed into one summary number.

The cycle

The calibration loop runs in four stages. Each pass addresses one or more flagged checks. The loop repeats until the remaining flags are either resolved or understood well enough to document as known limits.
